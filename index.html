A reader's guide to John Gray's 1992 bestseller

Men are from Mars, women are from Venus

The book's whole argument fits in one sentence: we love our partners the way we would want to be loved, and that is the problem. This page lays out the key ideas, lets you try the techniques, and ends with what the research actually says about the planets.

About 15 minutes to read. All summaries are paraphrased.

The premise

Gray opens with a fable. Martians spot Venusians through a telescope, fall in love, and fly over. The two peoples live happily for years because they know they are different and plan around it. Then they move to Earth, wake up with selective amnesia, and forget they were ever from different worlds. Every fight since, the story goes, comes from that forgetting.

Strip the fable away and the claim is practical. Each partner expects the other to think, feel and react the way they do. When the other doesn't, it gets read as not caring. Gray's fix is not to change your partner but to stop assuming they run on your operating system, and to learn what theirs needs.

Worth knowing before you read on. Gray says up front that these are generalisations, that some couples will recognise themselves in reverse (he calls it role reversal), and that he is not explaining why the differences exist. Read "Mars" and "Venus" as two coping styles that any person can lean towards. The last section of this page explains why that reading is closer to the evidence anyway.

    The phrase translator

    Gray's claim: the words are the same on both planets, the meanings aren't. Venusian complaints use big, poetic generalisations that Martians take literally. Martian one-liners are a request for space that Venusians hear as a brush-off.

    The points counter

    Gray says men keep score by size (a big gesture is worth thirty points) while women give every act of love one point, big or small. Add gestures and watch the two scoreboards drift apart.

    The asking coach

    Gray's rules for a request a Martian will say yes to: be direct, be brief, don't stack up reasons, and say "would you" rather than "could you" (he argues "could" questions ability, "would" invites a choice). Pick the version you think works best.

    Anatomy of a fight

    Gray's five-step loop for how a small upset becomes a row, using his own picnic example. Step through it, then see where it can be cut.

      The four ways people protect themselves (and why none works)

      Fight
      Attack first, win by intimidation. Trust drains away.
      Flight
      Retreat into the cave and never come back to the topic. A cold war.
      Fake
      Smile and say everything is fine. Resentment builds underneath.
      Fold
      Take all the blame to keep the peace, and slowly lose yourself.

      The Love Letter builder

      Gray's main tool for difficult feelings. Write privately, in order: anger, sadness, fear, regret, love, then a P.S. saying what you would like to hear back. The order matters to him; stopping at anger only stokes it. You don't have to send it.

      Back on Earth: what the evidence says
      
    
      The book is a set of observations from Gray's seminars, not research. It became one of the best-selling self-help books ever written, and its core metaphor, that men and women are categorically different, has since been tested properly. It did not hold up well.

      The main findings
      
    
      In 2005 Janet Shibley Hyde reviewed 46 meta-analyses of psychological sex differences. Her conclusion, the gender similarities hypothesis, is that men and women are alike on most psychological variables, with most differences close to zero or small. The marked exceptions were throwing distance, some aspects of sexuality, and physical aggression. She also found that differences shift with age and with the context they are measured in, and warned that inflated claims of difference can put couples off trying to resolve conflict.
      
    
      In 2013 Bobbi Carothers and Harry Reis tested whether psychological gender behaves like two categories or like a continuum. Physical traits such as height and strength do sort into two groups. Psychological traits, including empathy, intimacy and mate preferences, did not: they varied along a continuum. Their point is subtle and worth keeping: average differences are real, but you cannot predict an individual's style from their sex.
      
    
      On the messenger: Gray's PhD came from Columbia Pacific University, an unaccredited distance-learning school that a California court ordered closed around 2000.
      

    
      
      
      
        
      What still holds up
      
        
        
          
      • Validate before you fix. Hearing someone out before offering solutions is sound advice for anyone, of any gender.
        • 
          
      • Time-outs during a fight. Gray's "take a break, cool off, come back" maps onto Gottman's research on flooding, which recommends a break of at least 20 minutes and an agreed time to return.
        • 
          
      • Ask directly. Expecting a partner to read your mind is a reliable route to resentment.
        • 
          
      • Feelings before blame. "I don't like waiting when you're late" lands better than "How could you be so late?"
        • 
          
      • Small, frequent acts of care matter more than occasional grand gestures.
        • 
        
      
      
      
      
      
        
      What to read sceptically
      
        
        
          
      • The planets themselves. The evidence points to overlapping tendencies, not two species. Use the styles, drop the sexes.
        • 
          
      • Unsourced numbers. The 90/10 split, the 21 to 35 day self-esteem cycle and "hear it 200 times" are asserted, not referenced.
        • 
          
      • Health claims. The suggested link between unexpressed feelings and PMS, and the claim that most physical disease comes from emotional pain, are not supported.
        • 
          
      • Uneven burdens. Much of the advice asks women to manage men's moods: don't advise, don't criticise, ask sweetly, wait out the grumbling.
        • 
          
      • Not for unsafe relationships. Advice to "lie low" through a partner's anger and absorb his penalty points is harmful if there is intimidation or abuse. That needs professional help, not better phrasing.
        • 
          
      • Narrow frame. It assumes a heterosexual couple with 1990s American gender roles.
